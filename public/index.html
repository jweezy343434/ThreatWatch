Mariposa Botnet

CISA ICS Advisories • Mar 31, 2010 12:00 UTC

What Happened The Mariposa botnet was a sophisticated malware operation that infected millions of computers worldwide between 2008-2010. The botnet utilized the Butterfly Bot (also called Palevo) malware, which spread through removable drives, instant messaging, and peer-to-peer networks. Once infected, compromised machines became part of a distributed network controlled by cybercriminals who could steal credentials, financial data, and personal information from infected systems.

Impact Mariposa demonstrated the massive scale and economic damage botnets can inflict on critical infrastructure and organizations. The botnet compromised over 13 million IP addresses across 190+ countries, including systems belonging to major corporations, government agencies, and financial institutions. Infected machines experienced degraded performance, data theft, and served as launching points for additional attacks. The botnet’s ability to update itself and download additional malware made it particularly persistent and dangerous.

Actions Organizations should implement comprehensive endpoint protection with real-time malware detection and behavioral analysis. Disable autorun features on removable media and restrict USB device usage through group policies. Deploy network monitoring to identify suspicious botnet command-and-control communications. Maintain updated antivirus signatures and conduct regular system scans. Educate users about safe computing practices, particularly regarding removable media and file sharing. Establish incident response procedures for potential botnet infections, including network isolation protocols and forensic capabilities to assess compromise scope.

Impact This vulnerability undermines the fundamental security function of affected firewalls, potentially exposing internal networks to various attacks. Review firewall logs for suspicious HTTP traffic patterns that may indicate exploitation attempts. Update firewall rules to block unnecessary HTTP methods and implement strict content inspection policies. Organizations should also consider deploying web application firewalls as an additional layer of protection and conduct thorough security assessments of systems previously protected solely by the compromised firewall.

MyDoom.B Virus

US-CERT Current Activity • Jan 28, 2004 12:00 UTC

What Happened MyDoom.B is a mass-mailing computer worm that spreads through email attachments and peer-to-peer networks. The malware creates backdoors on infected systems, allowing remote access while also launching distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks against specific targets. It propagates by harvesting email addresses from infected computers and sending copies of itself to those contacts with deceptive subject lines and attachments.

Impact This worm poses significant risks to both individual users and organizations through data theft, system compromise, and network disruption. Infected computers become part of a botnet, consuming bandwidth and processing power while potentially exposing sensitive information. The DDoS capability can render targeted websites and services unavailable, disrupting business operations and online services critical to daily operations.

Actions Immediately update antivirus software and run full system scans on all computers. Block suspicious email attachments, especially executable files from unknown senders, and educate users about email security practices. Organizations should monitor network traffic for unusual outbound connections, implement email filtering solutions, and ensure firewalls are properly configured. If infection is suspected, disconnect affected systems from the network immediately and restore from clean backups after thorough cleaning.
